# Principle descriptions: rewrite review (2026-10-08)

## The audience determines which duty governs
`principle:audience-determines-governing-duty`

**Short, before**

> Who uses a system decides which law governs its accessibility: Title II and Section 504 for students and the public, Title I and Section 504 for employees and applicants.

**Short, after**

> Who uses a system decides which law governs its accessibility. Title II and Section 504 govern it for students and the public, and Title I and Section 504 govern it for employees and applicants.

**Full, before**

> The 2024 Title II rule covers the web content and mobile apps a public entity provides. Employment runs on a different track. Under 28 CFR 35.140, Title I and the EEOC's regulation govern employment by a public entity that Title I covers, and the rule states that meeting subpart H does not relieve those obligations. Title I's duty is individual: reasonable accommodation for a known limitation, found through an interactive process and bounded by undue hardship. EO 1111 runs separate interactive processes for students and for employees and applicants. Many campus systems serve both audiences, such as the learning management system faculty teach in and the HR platforms every employee uses. Procurement and remediation planning have to identify the audience before they can identify the duty.

**Full, after**

> The 2024 Title II rule covers the web content and mobile apps a public entity provides. It does not govern employment. Under 28 CFR 35.140, Title I and the EEOC's regulation at 29 CFR Part 1630 govern employment by a public entity that Title I covers, and DOJ says in the rule's preamble that complying with subpart H does not relieve a public entity of those Title I obligations.
> 
> Title I's duty runs to each person. The employer makes a reasonable accommodation for a known limitation, finds it through an interactive process, and may refuse only on a showing of undue hardship. EO 1111 runs one interactive process for students and a separate one for employees and applicants.
> 
> Many campus systems serve both groups. Faculty teach in the learning management system, and every employee uses the HR platforms. Before a campus can say which duty applies to a system it buys or fixes, it has to know who uses the system.

## Undue burden and fundamental alteration bound the duty
`principle:bounded-duty-burden-limits`

**Short, before**

> The obligation to remediate or to provide an equally effective alternative is bounded. It does not require a fundamental alteration of the program or undue financial and administrative burdens, though a residual duty to take other action remains.

**Short, after**

> The duty to remediate, or to provide an equally effective alternative, has limits. The institution need not take an action that would fundamentally alter a program or impose undue financial and administrative burdens, but it must still take other action that gives people with disabilities the program's benefits to the maximum extent possible.

**Full, before**

> The obligation to remediate or to provide an alternative is bounded. Where making a program accessible would fundamentally alter its nature or impose undue financial and administrative burdens, the institution is not required to take that particular action. The duty is bounded, not extinguished. It must still take any other action that would not produce such alteration or burden, and must ensure that people with disabilities receive the benefits and services it provides to the fullest extent possible. The limit is invoked by a documented determination made at the level of authority accountable for the program, supported by a written statement of reasons, never by an operating unit's assertion that the work is hard. This principle matters because without it the alternative-access commitment reads as absolute, implying that every non-conformant resource must be paired with an equally effective substitute however large the resource is. At sufficient scale no equivalent substitute can be constructed by the unit that subscribes to a resource, and the honest institutional answer is a documented bounded-duty determination with residual mitigation, not an unmeetable demand and not a silent exception. The limit reaches a particular action and not the standing obligation that made the action necessary. The institution is separately required to acquire, develop, and maintain technology that is accessible to begin with, and a burden determination about one remediation does not relax that requirement for the next acquisition. Providing one person an alternative does not discharge it either. The duty to make the program accessible and the duty to accommodate a particular person are parallel obligations, and satisfying the second leaves the first exactly where it was.

**Full, after**

> The duty to remediate or to provide an alternative has limits. Under 28 CFR 35.150(a)(3), 35.164 and 35.204, a public entity need not take an action it can show would fundamentally alter a service, program or activity, or would impose undue financial and administrative burdens. The limit excuses that action and leaves the duty in place. The entity must still take any other action that avoids the alteration or burden and still gives people with disabilities its benefits and services to the maximum extent possible.
> 
> The public entity carries the burden of proof. Under 35.204, the head of the entity or a designee decides, after considering all resources available to fund and operate the program, and records the decision in a written statement of reasons. An operating unit's report that the work is hard does not invoke the limit.
> 
> Without this limit, the alternative-access commitment reads as absolute: every non-conformant resource would need an equally effective substitute, however large the resource. For a large enough platform, the unit that subscribes to it cannot build an equivalent substitute. The honest answer then is a documented determination with the remaining mitigation listed, rather than a demand nobody can meet or an exception nobody records.
> 
> The limit applies to one action. It leaves in place the standing duty that made the action necessary. The institution must still develop, procure, maintain and use accessible technology, and a burden determination about one remediation does not relax that requirement for the next purchase. Accommodating one person does not discharge the duty either. The duty to make the program accessible and the duty to accommodate an individual run in parallel, and meeting the second leaves the first where it was.

## Capability maturity over binary compliance
`principle:capability-maturity-over-binary-compliance`

**Short, before**

> Accessibility is assessed as levels of organizational capability that improve and institutionalize over time, not as a fixed compliant/non-compliant state.

**Short, after**

> The CSU grades accessibility by levels of organizational capability that improve over time, not as compliant or non-compliant.

**Full, before**

> Accessibility is evaluated through graded levels of organizational capability rather than as a binary judgment of compliance. This is a substantive claim about what accessibility is: an institutionalized, continuously improving organizational capability, not a static endpoint that is either reached or not. It reflects a capability-maturity strategy aimed at reliably and sustainably delivering accessible services. This is the conceptual basis for representing status as graded maturity levels rather than a pass or fail flag, and for treating movement between levels as the unit of progress.

**Full, after**

> The CSU ATI policy grades each campus's success indicators by levels of organizational capability: Not Started, Initiated, Defined, Established, Managed and Optimized. The policy calls this a capabilities maturity strategy, and aims it at processes that deliver accessible services reliably and keep improving. Accessibility on this view is a capability the institution builds into its processes, not an endpoint it reaches once. A success indicator with a timeline must reach Established within it.
> 
> This is why the graph records status as a maturity level rather than a pass or fail flag, and why a move from one level to the next is the unit of progress.

## Closest to Capacity
`principle:closest-to-capacity`

**Short, before**

> Responsibility to remediate sits closest to the party with the capacity to remediate. Where that party has no such capacity, responsibility rises to the institution.

**Short, after**

> The party closest to the capacity to fix an asset is responsible for remediating it. Where that party cannot remediate, responsibility rises to the institution.

**Full, before**

> The heuristic has two steps, and only the first is intuitive. First, responsibility to remediate an interface or asset sits with the party closest to the capacity to fix it, normally the unit that builds, hosts, or maintains it. Second, and decisively, where the closest party cannot remediate, responsibility does not stop there. It rises to the institution. A unit that selects, subscribes to, and reports problems with a product it cannot modify holds stewardship without remediation capacity, and the duty passes upward rather than resting on a party unable to discharge it. Capacity is therefore decoupled from liability. The institution remains the liable party throughout, and the heuristic allocates work, not blame. A separate allocation runs alongside it: while capacity determines who remediates, the choice to deploy a non-conformant technology determines who bears the cost of accommodating the people it excludes. This is the conceptual basis for modeling stewardship (procure, develop, maintain, use) separately from remediation accountability, and for treating a stewarded asset with no remediating work as an elevation signal to be surfaced rather than a data gap to be filled.

**Full, after**

> The rule has two steps, and only the first is intuitive. First, the party closest to the capacity to fix an interface or asset is responsible for remediating it. That is normally the unit that builds, hosts or maintains it. Second, where that party cannot remediate, responsibility rises to the institution instead of stopping there. A unit that selects a product, subscribes to it and reports its problems, but cannot modify it, is a steward without the capacity to remediate, and the duty passes upward.
> 
> Capacity is separate from liability. Title II places the duty on the public entity, and Section 508 places it on the agency as a whole. Neither divides it among units inside. The institution stays liable throughout, so this rule allocates work, not blame. A second allocation runs alongside it: capacity decides who remediates, but the decision to deploy a non-conformant technology decides who pays to accommodate the people it excludes.
> 
> This is why the graph records stewardship (procures, develops, maintains, uses) separately from remediation accountability. It is also why a stewarded asset with no remediating implementation is an elevation signal to surface, not a data gap to fill.

## Conformance to an external technical standard
`principle:conformance-to-an-external-technical-standard`

**Short, before**

> Digital accessibility is measured against a fixed, externally defined technical standard incorporated by reference, not against an internal or subjective judgment of accessible enough.

**Short, after**

> Digital accessibility is measured against a fixed, published technical standard that the law incorporates by reference, not against an internal judgment of what is accessible enough.

**Full, before**

> Digital accessibility is measured against a fixed, externally maintained technical standard incorporated by reference, not against an internal or negotiable judgment of accessible enough. The significance is that conformance is assessment against a published, versioned, third-party criterion set the institution does not control and cannot quietly relax. A specific version is the binding floor. Later versions may be adopted but do not lower that floor. This grounds the treatment of the external standard as the authority that individuates components and defines what conformance means, the separation between a fixed standard layer and the institution's own assessment of where it stands against it, and the need to track which version governs as the standard evolves. The governing standard and version are carried on this principle's grounding edges, not in this text, so that a change of version does not require a change of principle.

**Full, after**

> The institution measures digital accessibility against a published, versioned technical standard that a third party maintains and the law incorporates by reference. The institution does not control that standard and cannot quietly relax it. The version the law names is the binding floor. The institution may adopt a later version, but a later version does not lower the floor.
> 
> Three parts of the model follow. The external standard decides what the components are and what conformance to each one means. The standard stays separate from the institution's own assessment of where it stands against it. The model records which version governs as the standard changes. It records the standard and version on this principle's derives_from edges, not in this text, so a new version does not need a new principle.
> 
> Conformance is the test the law applies, though it does not prove that a given person can use the resource. A student can meet a barrier in a product that passes every success criterion.

## Accessibility as continuous, sustained remediation
`principle:continuous-sustained-remediation`

**Short, before**

> Accessibility is an ongoing, multi-year process of incremental barrier removal, not a one-time achievement.

**Short, after**

> Accessibility is an ongoing, multi-year process of removing barriers, not a one-time achievement.

**Full, before**

> Accessibility barriers accumulate over years, and their removal often takes years to complete. The commitment is about the temporal nature of accessibility rather than any particular schedule: it is a sustained process that is never finished, so the institution's obligation is to achieve incremental barrier removal each year rather than to arrive at a single point of compliance. This is the basis for the annual evidence cycle, for tracking progress across academic years, and for treating the trajectory of an effort as meaningful independent of its absolute status.

**Full, after**

> Accessibility barriers build up over years, and removing them often takes years. The CSU ATI policy asks campuses to remove more barriers each year during that period. The commitment concerns how the work runs over time, not any particular schedule. The work is never finished, so the institution owes incremental barrier removal every year rather than a single point of compliance.
> 
> This is why campuses submit evidence on an annual cycle, why the graph tracks progress across academic years, and why the direction of an effort counts independently of its current status.

## Deference is earned through a documented, individualized process
`principle:deference-earned-through-documented-process`

**Short, before**

> Courts and OCR defer to an institution's judgment that a requirement is essential or that a change would fundamentally alter a program only when a record shows the responsible officials considered the individual and the alternatives.

**Short, after**

> Courts and OCR defer to an institution's judgment that a requirement is essential, or that a change would fundamentally alter a program, only when a record shows the responsible officials considered the individual and the alternatives.

**Full, before**

> Courts and OCR defer to a college's judgment that an academic requirement is essential or that a modification would fundamentally alter a program. That deference is conditional. The Section 504 regulation treats an academic requirement as non-discriminatory only if the recipient can demonstrate that it is essential, and the Title II regulation excuses a modification only if the public entity can demonstrate a fundamental alteration. Courts have read that burden as a process requirement. In Wynne v. Tufts University School of Medicine, the institution had to show that its relevant officials considered alternative means, their feasibility, cost, and effect on the program, and reached a rationally justifiable conclusion. An assertion with no record behind it receives no deference. Two consequences follow. A fundamental alteration determination about a course is made by the faculty and academic administrators responsible for it, after an individualized look at the student and the alternatives, and the record of that look is kept. A claim that an accessible version of a platform or material cannot be provided needs the same documented consideration, including whether technology has changed what is feasible. This principle concerns how a limit is established. Where the limits sit belongs to the principle that undue burden and fundamental alteration bound the duty.

**Full, after**

> Courts and OCR give substantial deference to a college's judgment that an academic requirement is essential or that a modification would fundamentally alter a program, but the deference is conditional. The Section 504 regulation at 34 CFR 104.44(a) treats an academic requirement as non-discriminatory only if the recipient can demonstrate that it is essential. The Title II regulation at 28 CFR 35.130(b)(7)(i) excuses a modification only if the public entity can demonstrate a fundamental alteration.
> 
> Courts have read that burden as a process requirement. In Wynne v. Tufts University School of Medicine (1st Cir. 1991 and 1992), Tufts had to show that its relevant officials considered alternative means, their feasibility, cost and effect on the program, and reached a rationally justifiable conclusion. An assertion with no record behind it gets no deference.
> 
> Two things follow. The faculty and academic administrators responsible for a course decide whether a change would fundamentally alter it, after an individualized look at the student and the alternatives, and they keep the record of that look. A claim that an accessible version of a platform or material cannot be provided needs the same documented consideration, including whether technology has changed what is feasible.
> 
> This principle governs how a limit is established. Where the limits sit is the subject of the principle that undue burden and fundamental alteration bound the duty.

## Equally effective access
`principle:equally-effective-access`

**Short, before**

> Accessible technology must afford the same result, benefit, or opportunity, not merely some alternative.

**Short, after**

> Accessible technology must give a person with a disability the same result, benefit or opportunity as others, not merely some alternative.

**Full, before**

> Accessible technology must afford the same result, benefit, or opportunity, not merely some alternative. Equal effectiveness is a conjunctive test of independent conditions rather than a gradient: an alternative qualifies only if it delivers the same information, engagement, and services, offers the same availability, can be used independently without additional assistance, imposes no disparate burden on the person using it, has substantially equivalent ease of use, and protects their privacy. Failing any one condition does not make an alternative mostly equally effective. It moves it into a lesser category carrying different and heavier obligations, which is why the outcome of an alternative-access assessment is one of three states rather than a score: equally effective, a non-equal alternative that may still require individual accommodation, or no viable alternative at all, where accommodation must be arranged case by case. Independence deserves particular weight. An alternative reachable only by asking someone for help is by definition not independent, so any request-gated route is categorically a lesser outcome no matter how well it is staffed.

**Full, after**

> Accessible technology must give the same result, benefit or opportunity, not merely some alternative. The Title II and Section 504 regulations prohibit an aid, benefit or service that is not as effective as the one provided to others (28 CFR 35.130(b)(1)(iii); 34 CFR 104.4(b)(1)(iii)), and the CSU ATI policy uses the same words.
> 
> Equal effectiveness is a set of independent conditions, not a scale. The CSU TAAP form tests an alternative against six. It must deliver the same information, engagement and services. It must offer the same availability. The person must be able to use it independently, without additional assistance. It must impose no disparate burden on that person. Its ease of use must be substantially equivalent. It must protect the person's privacy.
> 
> An alternative that fails one condition is not mostly equally effective. It falls into a lesser category with heavier obligations. That is why the form grades an alternative into one of three outcomes rather than a score: equally effective; a non-equal alternative that may still require individual accommodation; or no viable alternative, where each person's accommodation is arranged case by case.
> 
> Independence carries particular weight, and 28 CFR 35.160(b)(2) names it alongside privacy. An alternative a person can reach only by asking someone for help is not independent, so any request-gated route is a lesser outcome however well it is staffed.

## Federal funding brings Section 504 to every operation
`principle:federal-funding-reaches-every-operation`

**Short, before**

> Because the CSU receives federal financial assistance, Section 504 applies to all of its operations, not only to the program the funds pay for.

**Short, after**

> Because the CSU receives federal financial assistance, Section 504 applies to all of its operations, not only to the program the funds pay for.

**Full, before**

> Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination in any program or activity that receives federal financial assistance. The statute defines program or activity as all of the operations of a college, university, or public system of higher education. ED's regulation repeats the definition in 34 CFR 104.3(k). One federal grant therefore brings every CSU operation under Section 504, including operations the 2024 Title II rule excepts or does not reach. ED's regulation sets no web technical standard, so Section 504 reaches technology through nondiscrimination, academic adjustments and auxiliary aids rather than through a conformance date.

**Full, after**

> Section 504 prohibits disability discrimination in any program or activity that receives federal financial assistance. The statute, at 29 U.S.C. 794(b)(2)(A), defines program or activity as all of the operations of a college, university or public system of higher education. ED's regulation repeats the definition at 34 CFR 104.3(k).
> 
> One federal grant therefore brings every CSU operation under Section 504, including operations the 2024 Title II rule excepts or does not reach. ED's regulation sets no web technical standard. Section 504 reaches technology through its bans on discrimination and its requirements for academic adjustments and auxiliary aids, not through a conformance date.

## Accessibility is an institution-wide responsibility
`principle:institution-wide-responsibility`

**Short, before**

> The duty to provide accessible technology runs to the institution as a whole and requires leadership across the enterprise, not ownership by a single office.

**Short, after**

> The duty to provide accessible technology belongs to the institution as a whole and needs leadership across it. No single office owns it.

**Full, before**

> The duty to provide accessible technology belongs to the institution as a whole. Title II places it on the public entity, and Section 504 places it on the recipient of federal funds across all of its operations. Neither assigns it to an office within the institution. CSU policy says the same in its own terms: technology accessibility is an institution-wide responsibility that requires commitment and involvement from leadership across the enterprise. EO 1111 makes each campus president and the chancellor responsible for accessible technology programs with adequate administrative support and resources. Two consequences follow. No single office, whether disability services, information technology, or an accessibility team, owns the duty, and giving one of them the work does not move the obligation off the institution. Because the duty reaches every program, leadership commits resources across all of them rather than treating accessibility as one unit's mandate. This principle concerns where the duty sits. How the work is divided and coordinated among the units that carry it out belongs to the principle of shared responsibility, and who remediates a given asset belongs to the principle that responsibility sits closest to capacity.

**Full, after**

> The duty to provide accessible technology belongs to the institution as a whole. Title II places it on the public entity, and Section 504 places it on the recipient of federal funds across all of its operations. Neither assigns it to an office inside the institution.
> 
> CSU policy says the same. The ATI policy calls technology accessibility "an institution-wide responsibility that requires commitment and involvement from leadership across the enterprise." ## Flexibility for campus variation
`principle:local-adaptation-flexibility`

**Short, before**

> Implementation is locally adapted to each campus rather than uniformly imposed, within shared systemwide goals.

**Short, after**

> Each campus adapts how it carries out the ATI to its own conditions, within shared systemwide goals.

**Full, before**

> Implementation is locally adapted rather than uniformly imposed. Shared goals and success indicators are fixed, but the means of achieving them are adapted to local conditions, with those who carry out the work helping shape how it is done. Adaptation is bounded rather than open: a shared framework may be reshaped to fit local circumstance only so long as its core elements are retained, so that what varies is method and not substance. The boundary matters in both directions. A standard or threshold against which work is judged is substance and does not vary locally. The procedure for meeting and evidencing it is method and does. This is the basis for keeping the schema substrate-independent and multiply realizable, with shared structure in the model and local particulars in the data.

**Full, after**

> The CSU fixes the goals and success indicators and leaves each campus to adapt the means. The ATI policy says each campus faces its own challenges in implementing the ATI, and gives campuses adequate flexibility to manage implementation. The Executive Sponsors Steering Committee and the ATI Priority Area Communities of Practice helped design that approach, so the people who do the work shape how it is done.
> 
> The flexibility has a limit. A campus may reshape the shared framework to fit local conditions only while it keeps the core elements, so the method varies and the substance does not. The limit cuts both ways. A standard or threshold used to judge the work is substance, and it does not vary by campus: every campus must reach Established on the success indicators that carry a timeline. The procedure for meeting and evidencing it is method, and it does vary. Each campus also chooses which other indicators to work on according to its capacity.
> 
> This is why the schema holds the shared structure and the data holds each campus's particulars, so one model fits every campus.

## Notice is part of access, not administration around it
`principle:notice-as-part-of-access`

**Short, before**

> A remedy the affected person cannot discover is not a remedy. Disclosing known barriers and the route around them is constitutive of providing access, not paperwork that follows it.

**Short, after**

> A remedy the affected person cannot find is not a remedy. Telling people about known barriers and the way around them is part of providing access, not paperwork that follows it.

**Full, before**

> An alternative route only functions for someone who knows it exists before they need it. A plan held on file, unannounced, leaves the person it was written for to encounter the barrier unwarned, spend effort against it, and conclude that nothing is available. On that view disclosure is not administrative overhead attached to a remedy, it is a constituent of the remedy: an undiscoverable alternative and no alternative are indistinguishable in experience. Two obligations follow. Known barriers are stated proactively at the point where the technology is encountered, together with the alternative and a named route to help, rather than disclosed on request after someone has already been blocked. And the people positioned to relay that information, those who support and advise the affected population, are told directly rather than expected to find it. The commitment also has a limit: notice is owed to those who need it and to those who support them, which is not the same as broadcasting an inventory of institutional weaknesses to everyone.

**Full, after**

> An alternative route works only for someone who knows about it before they need it. A plan kept on file and never announced leaves the person it was written for to meet the barrier unwarned, spend effort on it, and conclude that nothing is available. Disclosure is part of the remedy, not overhead attached to it. To the person who needs it, an alternative nobody can find is the same as no alternative.
> 
> Two obligations follow. The institution states known barriers where people meet the technology, with the alternative and a named contact for help, instead of disclosing them on request after someone has been blocked. The institution also tells the people who support and advise the affected population directly, instead of expecting them to find out.
> 
> Notice has a limit. It is owed to the people who need it and to those who support them, which is not the same as publishing an inventory of institutional weaknesses to everyone.

## Conformance and accommodation are parallel duties
`principle:parallel-duties-conformance-and-accommodation`

**Short, before**

> Conforming to the technical standard does not discharge the duty to accommodate an individual, and accommodating an individual does not excuse content that fails the standard.

**Short, after**

> Conforming to the technical standard does not discharge the duty to accommodate an individual, and accommodating an individual does not excuse content that fails the standard.

**Full, before**

> Title II now carries two duties that run at the same time. The 2024 rule requires web content and mobile apps to conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA by a fixed date, whether or not anyone has asked. The effective communication duty in 28 CFR 35.160 and Section 504's auxiliary aids requirement still apply to each person who needs a different format, including for content the rule excepts. CSU policy gives the individual duty a procedure: EO 1111 requires an interactive process with each student who needs an accommodation. A campus that conforms has reduced how often it must accommodate, but it has not removed the duty. A campus that accommodates one person has not made the content conform for the next.

**Full, after**

> Title II now imposes two duties at the same time. The 2024 rule requires web content and mobile apps to conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA by a fixed date, whether or not anyone has asked. The effective communication duty in 28 CFR 35.160 and Section 504's auxiliary aids requirement at 34 CFR 104.44(d) still apply to each person who needs a different format, including for content the rule excepts. DOJ says in the rule's preamble that public entities must meet all of Title II's existing requirements both before and after their compliance date.
> 
> CSU policy gives the individual duty a procedure. EO 1111 requires the CSU to conduct an interactive process with each student with a disability to identify reasonable accommodations.
> 
> A campus that conforms has reduced how often it must accommodate, but it has not removed the duty. A campus that accommodates one person has not made the content conform for the next.

## Prioritization by impact under finite resources
`principle:prioritization-by-impact`

**Short, before**

> Because resources are finite, accessibility work is triaged toward the barriers with the greatest impact rather than pursued uniformly.

**Short, after**

> Because resources are finite, campuses direct accessibility work at the barriers with the greatest impact rather than spreading it evenly.

**Full, before**

> Accessibility work is triaged toward the barriers with the greatest impact rather than pursued uniformly, in explicit recognition that staffing, time, and tools are finite in any given period. The underlying commitment is a value choice about allocation, made through an impact, probability, and capacity framework. Two corollaries follow. Effort spent uniformly is effort withheld from where it would matter most. And the access cost of a control is weighable on the same ledger as the barriers that control exists to prevent, since a measure that withholds a resource from everyone imposes its own loss of access. This is the basis for priority levels on tracked work and connects to the minimal-impact reasoning that governs whether non-conforming technology may remain in use while remediation proceeds.

**Full, after**

> Staffing, time and tools are finite in any year, so the CSU ATI policy asks campuses to select work that targets the barriers with the greatest impact. Campuses weigh impact, probability and capacity using the ATI Prioritization Framework or a comparable process.
> 
> Two corollaries follow. Effort spread evenly is effort withheld from where it would matter most. A control that withholds a resource from everyone also causes its own loss of access, so its access cost is weighed against the barriers it exists to prevent.
> 
> This is why tracked work carries a priority level. The principle connects to the minimal-impact test in 28 CFR 35.205, under which a public entity meets the Title II rule despite non-conformance whose effect is so minimal that people with disabilities can still use the content on substantially equal terms.

## Program accessibility is a proactive, systemic duty
`principle:program-accessibility-as-proactive-duty`

**Short, before**

> The institution must make its programs, services, and activities accessible in advance, independent of any individual request.

**Short, after**

> The institution must make its programs, services and activities accessible in advance, whether or not anyone has asked.

**Full, before**

> The institution must make its programs, services, and activities readily accessible to and usable by people with disabilities. The defining feature of this duty is that it is proactive and systemic: accessibility must be built into the institution's digital presence ahead of need and for the general population of users, not produced reactively in response to a particular person's request. Program accessibility is a standing obligation on the interfaces and assets the institution offers, distinct from and never satisfied by individual accommodation. The unit at which the duty is measured, and the latitude in method that follows from it, belong to the principle that access is measured across the program. Compliance dates and the instruments that set them belong on the governance nodes this principle derives from, never in this text.

**Full, after**

> The institution must make its programs, services and activities readily accessible to and usable by people with disabilities (28 CFR 35.150(a); 28 CFR 35.200(a)). The duty is anticipatory and systemic. The institution builds accessibility into its digital presence before anyone needs it and for everyone who uses it, instead of producing it in response to one person's request. Program accessibility is a standing obligation on the interfaces and assets the institution offers. It is separate from individual accommodation, and accommodation never satisfies it.
> 
> The 2024 Title II rule makes the anticipatory duty explicit for web content and mobile apps. Outside that rule, Colker argues that the ADA still lets an entity wait for a request, so the sources ground this principle partly as a position the institution takes and not only as a reading of the statute.
> 
> The unit at which the duty is measured, and the choice of method that follows, belong to the principle that access is measured across the program. Compliance dates and the instruments that set them live on the governance nodes this principle derives from, not in this text.

## Access is measured across the program, with latitude in method
`principle:program-viewed-in-its-entirety`

**Short, before**

> The duty is to make each program accessible when viewed in its entirety, so the institution may choose among effective methods instead of making every facility or artifact accessible, with priority to the most integrated setting. For web content and mobile apps, the 2024 Title II rule narrows that latitude.

**Short, after**

> The duty is to make each program accessible when viewed in its entirety, so the institution may choose among effective methods instead of making every facility or artifact accessible, giving priority to the most integrated setting. The 2024 Title II rule narrows that choice for web content and mobile apps.

**Full, before**

> Section 504 and Title II measure access at the level of the program. ED's regulation requires a recipient to operate its program so that, when each part is viewed in its entirety, it is readily accessible. It does not require every existing facility, or every part of one, to be accessible. A recipient may use any effective method, such as moving a class to an accessible building, and need not make structural changes where other methods work. Title II's regulation at 28 CFR 35.150 uses the same test and gives priority to methods that offer the most integrated setting appropriate. This latitude is why an alternative format or route can satisfy the duty. The 2024 Title II rule narrows it for web content and mobile apps: content must conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA, and conforming alternate versions are allowed only where direct accessibility is not possible for technical or legal reasons. The program-level test still governs what falls outside the rule's technical scope, and it still shapes how a campus answers an individual's request.

**Full, after**

## Remedy proportionate to barrier severity
`principle:proportionate-remedy`

**Short, before**

> The weight of the remedial instrument must match the severity of the barrier. Formal alternative-access planning attaches to barriers that block essential functions, not to every conformance imperfection.

**Short, after**

> The weight of the remedy must match the severity of the barrier. Formal alternative-access planning applies to barriers that block essential functions, not to every conformance flaw.

**Full, before**

> Not every departure from a technical standard calls for the same response. Formal alternative-access planning is the institution's answer to barriers severe enough to prevent people from performing essential functions. Conformance failures that do not prevent core functionality do not trigger it, and treating them as though they do is not extra rigor but a misallocation. The reason is practical and follows from finite capacity: a process applied uniformly to every imperfection consumes the attention that severe barriers require, and produces documentation whose volume obscures rather than demonstrates the institution's actual position. It also has a legal edge. Non-conformance whose impact on access is minimal, leaving people able to obtain the same information, engage in the same interactions, complete the same transactions, and otherwise benefit from the same services, is treated differently from non-conformance that excludes. Proportionality is what keeps the distinction operative rather than collapsing every finding into a single undifferentiated category.

**Full, after**

> Not every departure from a technical standard calls for the same response. The institution uses formal alternative-access planning for barriers severe enough to stop people performing essential functions, and the CSU TAAP process describes the plan as an interim measure for known barriers that affect core functionality. A conformance failure that leaves core functions usable does not trigger it. Treating it as though it did misallocates effort and adds no rigor.
> 
> The reason follows from finite capacity. A process applied to every imperfection uses up the attention severe barriers need. Its volume of documentation also hides the institution's actual position instead of showing it.
> 
> The distinction has a legal basis. Under 28 CFR 35.205, a public entity meets the Title II rule despite non-conformance whose effect is so minimal that people with disabilities can still obtain the same information, engage in the same interactions, complete the same transactions and receive the same services, with substantially equivalent timeliness, privacy, independence and ease of use. The entity must be able to demonstrate that. Proportionality keeps minimal-impact non-conformance apart from non-conformance that excludes people, instead of collapsing every finding into one category.

## Shared responsibility requiring coordination
`principle:shared-responsibility-requiring-coordination`

**Short, before**

> Meeting the accessibility duty requires coordinated, ongoing effort across distributed actors, which a coordinating function exists to provide.

**Short, after**

> Meeting the accessibility duty takes coordinated, ongoing work by many units, and a coordinating function exists to organize it.

**Full, before**

> Distinct from the principle that the duty sits with the institution as a whole, this principle concerns how the duty is met: accessibility is a shared responsibility that requires a coordinated, ongoing effort across many actors to succeed. Because no single unit produces institutional accessibility on its own, the work is a collective action problem that needs a coordinating function. This is the conceptual root of the coordinating/second-line role and of the distinction between line authority (held by the units that build and operate technology) and functional authority (held by the accessibility function accountable for an outcome it does not directly control). It justifies attaching working-group accountability to the work and maintaining a coordinating body across distributed owners.

**Full, after**

> The institution-wide principle says where the duty sits. This one says how the duty is met. EO 1111 calls accessibility a shared responsibility that requires a coordinated, ongoing campus-wide and systemwide effort, and the CSU ATI policy repeats the sentence for information technology. No single unit produces institutional accessibility on its own, so the work is a collective action problem that needs someone coordinating it.
> 
> Law and policy name that someone. Title II requires a public entity with 50 or more employees to designate a coordinator (28 CFR 35.107(a)), and Section 504 requires one at 15 or more (34 CFR 104.7(a)). EO 1111 has each president and the chancellor appoint an ATI Executive Sponsor, who convenes a campus ATI Steering Committee. The 2024 CSU memorandum asks each campus to confirm an ATI Coordinator, separate from the Executive Sponsor, to oversee daily compliance.
> 
> The principle is the root of the coordinating role in the model. It is also the root of the distinction between line authority, which the units that build and operate technology hold, and functional authority, which the accessibility function holds while being accountable for an outcome it does not directly control. It is why working groups carry accountability for work, and why a coordinating body spans the distributed owners.

## California adopts Section 508 as the state's technology standard
`principle:state-law-adopts-the-federal-standard`

**Short, before**

> California law makes the federal Section 508 standard the accessibility requirement for state entities, including the CSU, when they develop, procure, maintain, or use technology.

**Short, after**

> California law makes the federal Section 508 standard the accessibility requirement for state entities when they develop, procure, maintain or use technology. CSU policy applies it to the CSU.

**Full, before**

> Section 508 binds federal agencies, not states. California imports it. Government Code 7405 requires state governmental entities that develop, procure, maintain, or use electronic or information technology to comply with Section 508 and its regulations. The CSU's ATI policy states that Government Code 11135 applies Section 508 to the CSU. This makes Section 508 the standard for CSU procurement, separate from the Title II rule's WCAG 2.1 Level AA requirement for web content and mobile apps. The two overlap because the revised Section 508 standards incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level AA, but they are different requirements with different scopes and no shared deadline.

**Full, after**

> Section 508 binds federal agencies, not states, but California imports it. Government Code 7405(a) requires state governmental entities that develop, procure, maintain or use electronic or information technology to comply with Section 508 and its regulations. Section 7405(b) requires contractors with entities subject to Government Code 11135, which names the CSU, to agree to respond to and resolve accessibility complaints about their products.
> 
> The CSU ATI policy applies Section 508 to the CSU and cites Government Code 11135 for it. The current text of 11135 adopts the Title II standard and does not mention Section 508. Section 7405 is the provision that adopts it.
> 
> Section 508 is therefore the CSU's standard for technology it develops, procures, maintains or uses. It is separate from the Title II rule's WCAG 2.1 Level AA requirement for web content and mobile apps. The two overlap, because the revised Section 508 standards incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level AA, but they are different requirements with different scopes, and only the Title II rule sets a deadline.

## Time-bound alternative access when full conformance is not yet achieved
`principle:time-bound-alternative-when-not-conformant`

**Short, before**

> Where program content cannot meet the standard, the institution provides a documented, time-bound equally effective alternative while it remediates, rather than treating non-conformance as acceptable.

**Short, after**

> Where program content cannot yet meet the standard, the institution provides a documented, time-limited, equally effective alternative while it remediates, and does not treat the non-conformance as acceptable.

**Full, before**

> Where content does not meet the governing standard, the institution's obligation is to provide an effective means of access while remediation proceeds, with the exception kept time-bound rather than permanent. Non-conformance is a temporary, managed state carrying an affirmative duty to provide alternative access, not a resting state. The time-bound quality is substantive, not procedural. An exception's validity is CONDITIONAL on demonstrable remediation progress by the party with the capacity to remediate. Where a review finds no measurable progress, the exception lapses rather than renewing by default, and continuing to rely on the non-conformant technology becomes an explicit acceptance of residual risk that must be recorded at the level of authority that bears it. An exception that renews indefinitely without evidence of progress has become a permanent exemption wearing a temporary label. This grounds the alternate-access-plan instrument, its annual review and graded outcomes, the modeling of non-conformance as an active condition requiring a covering plan, and the elevation signal when a non-conformant asset has no remediating work or covering alternative.

**Full, after**

> Where content does not meet the governing standard, the institution must provide an effective way to access it while remediation proceeds, and it keeps the exception temporary. Non-conformance is a temporary state the institution manages, with an affirmative duty to provide alternative access. It is not a resting state. The CSU carries this out through its Temporary Alternate Access Plan (TAAP), formerly the Equally Effective Access Plan. The ATI policy requires a plan for any ICT product that is not fully Section 508 compliant.
> 
> The time limit is a condition. An exception stays valid only while the party with the capacity to remediate shows progress. If a review finds no measurable progress, the exception lapses instead of renewing by default. Continuing to use the technology then becomes an explicit acceptance of residual risk, recorded by the authority that bears the risk. TAAP version 3.3 requires formal executive acknowledgment when a plan continues without vendor progress. An exception that renews every year without evidence of progress is a permanent exemption with a temporary label.
> 
> An alternative does not make the content compliant. TAAP version 3.3 says a plan does not indicate Title II compliance. The 2024 Title II rule allows a conforming alternate version only where technical or legal limits make direct accessibility impossible (28 CFR 35.202), and it provides no general interim alternative while content is remediated. The plan manages access and risk during remediation. It does not cure the non-conformance.
> 
> This principle grounds the TAAP in the model: its annual review and graded outcomes, the treatment of non-conformance as an active condition that needs a covering plan, and the elevation signal when a non-conformant asset has neither remediating work nor a covering alternative.

## Universal Design reduces accommodation
`principle:universal-design-over-accommodation`

**Short, before**

> Designing for accessibility up front reduces the need for reactive individual accommodations.

**Short, after**

> Designing for accessibility up front reduces the need for individual accommodations after the fact.

**Full, before**

> Accessibility built into technology when it is designed, acquired, or configured serves everyone who encounters it, and requires nobody to identify themselves as needing it. Accommodation runs the other way. It is arranged for one person, after they have met a barrier, and only once they have asked for it. The two are not alternatives of equal standing. The institution is required to acquire and maintain technology that is accessible to begin with, and accommodation is what remains necessary where that requirement has not been met. A program that relies on accommodation to deliver access has not reduced its obligation. It has converted a standing obligation into a recurring one, and moved the cost of access onto the people the technology excludes. Designing and buying for accessibility up front is therefore not simply the more efficient option. It is what the obligation asks for, and accommodation is the residue left when it has not been done.

**Full, after**

> Accessibility built into technology when it is designed, acquired or configured serves everyone who meets it, and nobody has to identify as needing it. Accommodation works the other way. The institution arranges it for one person, after that person has met a barrier and asked. The CSU ATI policy states that implementing Universal Design principles should reduce the need for, and costs of, individual accommodations for inaccessible technology products.
> 
> The two are not equal options. Section 508 and Government Code 7405 apply the accessibility requirement when technology is developed, procured, maintained or used, before anyone asks. A program that relies on accommodation to deliver access has not reduced its obligation. It has turned a standing obligation into a recurring one, and moved the cost of access onto the people the technology excludes.
> 
> Design does not remove the need for accommodation. Some needs conflict, so a design that serves one group can fail another, and an individual response stays necessary alongside it. Studies have not shown that Universal Design for Learning improves educational outcomes, which qualifies claims about teaching but not claims about removing barriers from technology.

## Procurement as an accessibility lever
`principle:vendor-leverage-procurement-as-accessibility-lever`

**Short, before**

> The institution advances accessibility upstream at acquisition, shaping vendor products through partnerships and the procurement process.

**Short, after**

> The institution advances accessibility at the point of purchase, shaping vendor products through the procurement process and partnerships with vendors.

**Full, before**

> Accessibility can and should be advanced upstream, at the point of acquisition, rather than only remediated after a product is in use. The institution's purchasing power is a legitimate instrument for improving the accessibility of the market it buys from, exercised through the procurement process and through partnership with vendors and publishers. The lever has a cost, and the principle is only accurate when that cost is stated. Gatekeeping can consume the thing it protects. A review that blocks acquisition without producing product-specific findings, a stated threshold, or a path to approval stops functioning as a lever and becomes a blockade, and the resulting loss of access is itself an access harm. Leverage is also unevenly distributed. It is strongest for competitive, substitutable products and weakest for sole-source or centrally licensed platforms already embedded across the market, where declining to buy forfeits the resource without moving the vendor. The commitment is therefore not to gatekeeping as such, but to upstream influence exercised through findings, thresholds, and timelines the reviewed unit can act on.

**Full, after**

> The institution can advance accessibility at the point of purchase instead of only remediating a product after it is in use. The CSU ATI policy says the CSU seeks to improve product accessibility through partnerships and by leveraging the procurement process. In the CSU ATI procurement process, the ATI reviewer approves or denies an acquisition and sends contract requirements to the buyer: a vendor accessibility roadmap, an accessibility statement in the product, and a commitment to current Accessibility Conformance Reports. Government Code 7405 applies Section 508 when a state entity procures technology, and requires contractors to agree to resolve accessibility complaints about their products.
> 
> The lever has a cost, and the principle is accurate only when the cost is stated. A review that blocks a purchase without product-specific findings, a stated threshold or a path to approval stops working as a lever and becomes a blockade. The access lost is itself an access harm.
> 
> Leverage is also uneven. It is strongest for competitive products a campus could replace, and weakest for sole-source or centrally licensed platforms already embedded across the market, where declining to buy gives up the resource without moving the vendor. The commitment is to influence vendors upstream through findings, thresholds and timelines the reviewed unit can act on, not to gatekeeping as such.
